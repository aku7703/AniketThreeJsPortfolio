Norms: three ways to measure a weight vector

Source: Day 13 pp. 2–8 (norms, quick ICA); quiz7 reminder sheet. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A model with huge weights fits noise. Before we can charge a model for being “large”, we need a number that says how large a weight vector is. Different norms measure size differently, and that difference is what makes ridge and lasso behave differently.

You need this first

Absolute value ∣a∣|a|∣a∣ drops the sign. A square a2a^2a2 is never negative. A sum ∑j\sum_j∑j​ runs over every weight wjw_jwj​. The bias/intercept is usually not penalized.

The idea in plain words

Day 13’s pictures: a cab drives the grid, so it pays blocks east/west plus blocks north/south (L1). A bird flies straight (L2). A screw fits only if every dimension fits, so the worst dimension decides (L∞). All three are zero only for the zero vector and scale with ∣c∣|c|∣c∣.

Ridge and lasso: fit plus a price on size

Source: Day 13 pp. 1, 9–17; Day 12 (overfitting); quiz7 reminder sheet; HW7 prog Q1–Q2. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

Ordinary least squares only asks “how well do you fit the training data?” With many or correlated features it can fit noise using wild weights. Regularization adds a second question, “how large are your weights?”, and λ sets the exchange rate between the two.

You need this first

MSE is the mean of squared residuals. A derivative set to zero finds a minimum of a smooth bowl. λ ≥ 0 is chosen by cross-validation, never by training error.

The idea in plain words

Day 13: J(w) = (how badly w fits) + (how large w is). λ=0 gives OLS. Larger λ shrinks weights toward 0, trading a little bias for less variance. Ridge (L2²) shrinks every weight smoothly but almost never to exactly 0. Lasso (L1) can set weights to exactly 0, so it selects features.

HW7 Q1: lasso as a feature detective

Source: HW7 prog Q1 and its solution notebook; Day 13 pp. 12–17. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A secret polynomial uses 3 of 20 powers. OLS on all 20 cannot tell you which three; lasso can, because it pays for every non-zero weight.

You need this first

Standardizing a column subtracts its mean and divides by its standard deviation. Correlation near 1 means two columns are almost copies.

The idea in plain words

Build x1,…,x20x^1,\dots,x^{20}x1,…,x20, standardize, sweep λ, pick the λ with lowest cross-validated MSE, fit lasso, read the three largest |weights|, then refit OLS on only those three because lasso’s kept weights are shrunk.

Now read the math

The official result: lowest CV MSE at λ=10−3\lambda=10^{-3}λ=10−3; lasso’s largest weights sit on x2,x3,x19x^2,x^3,x^{19}x2,x3,x19; OLS on those gives

y≈2.99x2−2.02x3+4.02x19(secret: 3x2−2x3+4x19)y\approx 2.99x^2-2.02x^3+4.02x^{19}\quad(\text{secret: }3x^2-2x^3+4x^{19})y≈2.99x2−2.02x3+4.02x19(secret: 3x2−2x3+4x19)

A worked example, every step

Q: Why not fit OLS on all 20 and keep the three largest weights? On [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1] the powers are near-copies (x17x^{17}x17 vs x19x^{19}x19: correlation ≈ 0.999). OLS’s weights swing wildly (±160, alternating signs) and its three largest name the wrong powers (x12,x16,x18x^{12},x^{16},x^{18}x12,x16,x18). Lasso charges for every non-zero weight, so it explains yyy with as few features as it can.

Q: Lasso kept more than three weights. Why? Lowest CV MSE picks the best λ for prediction, not for selection: two tiny extra weights (x5x^5x5, x20x^{20}x20) lower the MSE a hair. They are tiny, so the true three still stand out.

Q: Why refit OLS? Lasso shrinks the weights it keeps, so they underestimate a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c. Lasso chooses which; OLS on the chosen few estimates how much.

Q: Why standardize? The penalty charges every weight equally. If one column has a tiny scale, it needs a huge weight for the same effect and gets punished unfairly; on a shared scale, comparing weight sizes is fair.

HW7 Q2: correlated features, bootstrap and ridge

Source: HW7 prog Q2 and its solution notebook; Day 13 (very different w, same MSE); Day 14 p. 18 (bootstrap). Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

Living area and number of rooms move together (correlation 0.98). OLS cannot decide how to split credit between them, and a realtor’s question depends on exactly that split.

You need this first

A bootstrap sample draws N rows with replacement (rows can repeat). Standard deviation across bootstrap fits measures how much a weight would change with different data.

The idea in plain words

Source: Day 14 pp. 2–12 (penguin ICA, Gini, car splits); HW7 P3; quiz7 reminder sheet. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A decision tree asks yes/no questions like “x₀ < 3.5?”. To choose the best question it needs a score for how mixed the labels in each group are. Gini is that score.

You need this first

A class proportion pkp_kpk​ is the class count divided by the node’s sample count. A weighted average multiplies each group’s value by its share of samples.

The idea in plain words

Day 14’s penguin game: copy a random penguin’s species as your guess for another random penguin. Gini is the probability that guess is wrong. Pure node → 0. Evenly mixed K classes → 1−1/K1-1/K1−1/K. A split is good if the sample-weighted Gini of the children is low, i.e. the reduction is large.

Forests and feature importance: when to trust the bars

Source: Day 14 pp. 14–23 (bagging, forests, importance); HW7 P5. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

One deep tree overfits. A random forest averages many varied trees. Its importance plot is a popular “explanation”, and HW7 P5 tests whether you know what it can and cannot say.

You need this first

Bagging: each tree trains on a bootstrap sample. Forests also consider a random subset of features at each split, then vote (classification) or average (regression).

The idea in plain words

Course definition: a feature’s importance = total Gini decrease from every split on that feature, across all trees, as a % of the forest’s total decrease. It measures how much the forest used the feature. It has no direction, is not a correlation, and is not causation.

Now read the math

Three-step reading rule:

  1. Does the classifier work? Compare CV accuracy to the baseline “always guess the majority class” = max(p, 1−p). If CV accuracy is not clearly above that, set the plot aside: nobody cares what a useless model relies on.
  2. If it works, name the top features and their share (“about two thirds of the Gini decrease comes from…”).
  3. Never read direction or causation from a bar. To learn how a feature relates to y, look at the data.

A worked example, every step

FRESH-question drill: explain, don’t just compute

Source: Targets the second (FRESH) question, where your earlier quizzes lost the most points. Source links

Your earlier quizzes lost more points on the second, FRESH question than on the FAMILIAR one. FRESH questions reuse the HW math in a new story and ask for a sentence of reasoning. Answer each in ≤ 2 sentences on paper, then compare. Name the mechanism, give a number from the problem, and state the conclusion.

1. Standardize first

Feature A is in meters (0–2), B in grams (0–5000). Lasso zeros A. Should we conclude A is useless?

Full worked solution

No. Without standardizing, A needs a much larger weight for the same effect, and the L1 penalty charges that heavily, so lasso drops it for its units. Standardize both columns, then compare.

2. Training error vs λ

Training MSE rises steadily as λ goes 0 → 100. Is λ = 0 best?

Full worked solution

Not necessarily: a penalty can only worsen the training fit, so training MSE always prefers λ = 0. Choose λ by cross-validated MSE, which measures error on unseen rows.

3. Bad baseline

Fraud is 2% of transactions; the forest’s CV accuracy is 97%. Read the importance plot?

Full worked solution

No: always guessing “not fraud” already scores 98%, so 97% is below the baseline and the forest has not learned anything useful. Set the plot aside.

4. Pure tiny child

Split S puts 1 sample in a pure left child and 99 in a right child with Gini 0.49 (parent 0.50). Split T makes two children of 50 with Gini 0.30 each. Which is better?

Full worked solution

T. S’s weighted Gini is 0.01(0)+0.99(0.49)=0.4850.01(0)+0.99(0.49)=0.4850.01(0)+0.99(0.49)=0.485 (reduction 0.015); T’s is 0.300.300.30 (reduction 0.20). A tiny pure child carries tiny weight.

5. Ridge vs lasso choice

You suspect only 5 of 300 sensors matter. Ridge or lasso? Why?

Full worked solution

Lasso: its L1 penalty can set weights exactly to zero, so it selects the few sensors that matter; ridge shrinks all 300 but keeps them non-zero. Refit OLS on the selected sensors to undo lasso’s shrinkage.

6. Bootstrap swing

Across 200 bootstrap fits, wagew_{\text{age}}wage​ has std 3.1 and wage+wyears employedw_{\text{age}}+w_{\text{years employed}}wage​+wyears employed​ has std 0.2. What is going on?

Full worked solution

Age and years employed are highly correlated, so predictions depend on their sum, which the data pin down (std 0.2), while the split between them is barely determined (std 3.1). Do not interpret either weight alone.
