Read a model: rows, features and a bias

Source: Day 11 pp. 2, 11–13; Day 12 pp. 16–17; HW6 pp. 1, 3–4. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A fraud filter needs a compact way to combine purchase amount and an abroad flag. A weighted score lets the same recipe process every transaction. First we must learn to read that recipe.

You need this first

A scalar is one number. A vector is an ordered list. A matrix is a table of numbers. A feature is a measured input; a label is the answer we hope to predict. Here y=1 and y=0 name classes, not good and bad. A hat means an estimate: y is truth, while ŷ is the predicted class. The subscript i names a sample (row); j names a feature (column).

The idea in plain words

Each row of X describes one sample. Each column has one consistent meaning. A weight says how strongly its feature affects a score. The special input x₀=1 makes a fixed starting score possible. That input is the bias feature; w₀ is its weight (the intercept). A score is not yet a probability.

Now read the math

A dot product means “multiply matching entries, then add.” A superscript T (or ⊤) turns a column into a row; it does not change its entries.

x=[120],w=[−30.52]x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad w=\begin{bmatrix}-3\\0.5\\2\end{bmatrix}x=​120​​,w=​−30.52​​

Read x as “bias one, amount two hundreds of dollars, abroad flag zero.” Read w as “intercept minus three, amount weight one half, abroad weight two.”

z=w⊤x=w⋅x=(−3)(1)+(0.5)(2)+(2)(0)=−3+1+0=−2z=w^\top x=w\cdot x=(-3)(1)+(0.5)(2)+(2)(0)=-3+1+0=-2z=w⊤x=w⋅x=(−3)(1)+(0.5)(2)+(2)(0)=−3+1+0=−2

z names the score. For all n rows at once, Xw gives n scores. If X is n by d and w is d by 1, their product is n by 1. Each entry still uses the same dot product.

A tiny example, every step

HW6 row 2 is x=[1,4,1]. Compute each product: −3×1=−3, 0.5×4=2, 2×1=2. Add: −3+2+2=1. The abroad flag contributes 2 to the score; it does not multiply the whole score by 2. Replacing the bias 1 by 0 would incorrectly remove the intercept.

Check yourself

Why insert x₀=1?

X has 4 rows and 3 columns; w has 3 entries. How many scores does Xw contain?

Common confusions and the next connection

The dot product is a sum, not entrywise output. Do not confuse sample xᵢ with feature xⱼ. Next we learn what probability and odds mean before interpreting that score.

Probability, odds and Bayes from counts

Source: Day 11 pp. 5–6, 15; HW6 p. 2 (probability prerequisites unpacked here). Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A priority flag on a phone notification may make it seem important, but the flag alone cannot tell us its probability of truly needing attention. We must combine how common important notifications are with how often each kind gets flagged. Odds make that update a multiplication.

You need this first

A probability is the fraction of a group having a property. P(A|B) reads “probability of A among cases where B is true.” The bar changes the group we count within. P(A|B) usually differs from P(B|A). A complement is “not A,” so its probability is 1−P(A). In HW6 phishing and legitimate exhaust the possibilities. A joint probability P(A,B) counts both properties together.

The idea in plain words

Fresh teaching example, not course data: imagine 100 notifications, 20 important and 80 routine. A priority flag appears on 10 important and 20 routine notifications. Among the 30 flagged notifications, 10 are important. Counting within this new group is Bayes’ rule.

Now read the math

Let p be a class-1 probability. Odds O compares class 1 to class 0. Odds 3 means “three class-1 cases per class-0 case,” not 300% probability.

O=p1−pO=\frac{p}{1-p}O=1−pp​

Convert odds back, one step at a time:

O(1−p)=p  ⇒  O−Op=p  ⇒  O=p(1+O)  ⇒  p=O1+OO(1-p)=p\;\Rightarrow\;O-Op=p\;\Rightarrow\;O=p(1+O)\;\Rightarrow\;p=\frac{O}{1+O}O(1−p)=p⇒O−Op=p⇒O=p(1+O)⇒p=1+OO​

Let E be the observed evidence. Multiplication gives P(E,y)=P(E|y)P(y). Divide each joint probability by P(E) to restrict to the evidence group:

P(y=1∣E)=P(E∣y=1)P(y=1)P(E)P(y=1|E)=\frac{P(E|y=1)P(y=1)}{P(E)}P(y=1∣E)=P(E)P(E∣y=1)P(y=1)​

Divide by the corresponding class-0 expression. The same P(E) cancels:

P(1∣E)P(0∣E)=P(E∣1)P(E∣0)P(1)P(0)\frac{P(1|E)}{P(0|E)}=\frac{P(E|1)}{P(E|0)}\frac{P(1)}{P(0)}P(0∣E)P(1∣E)​=P(E∣0)P(E∣1)​P(0)P(1)​

This says “posterior odds = evidence likelihood ratio × prior odds.” Prior means before observing E; posterior means after. No independence assumption between E and y is being made.

A tiny example, every step

For this teaching example, prior odds=20/80=1/4. The flag likelihood ratio is (10/20)/(20/80)=(1/2)/(1/4)=2. Posterior odds=2×1/4=1/2. Convert to probability: (1/2)/(1+1/2)=(1/2)/(3/2)=1/3. Count check: 10/(10+20)=1/3. “Half of important notifications are flagged” is not “half of flagged notifications are important.” Without a flag, counts are 10 important and 60 routine: odds=1/6 and probability=1/7.

Check yourself

Odds 3 corresponds to which probability?

60% of phishing emails contain urgent. Is that P(phishing | urgent)?

An evidence likelihood ratio of 1 does what?

Common confusions and the next connection

Probability uses total cases in the denominator; odds uses opposite-class cases. Next we take a logarithm of odds so multiplicative evidence can become an additive score.

From log odds to a probability

Source: Day 11 pp. 3–15; HW6 pp. 1–2. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A straight regression line can predict values below zero or above one. For a fraud probability that is impossible. Logistic regression keeps a simple weighted score, then bends it into the probability range. Like a dimmer approaching full brightness, its output saturates instead of growing forever.

You need this first

An exponential eᶻ is always positive; e≈2.71828. Multiplication of exponentials adds exponents: eᵃeᵇ=eᵃ⁺ᵇ. The natural log ln reverses an exponential: ln(eᶻ)=z and e^(ln a)=a for a>0. ln(ab)=ln a+ln b. A log can be negative: ln(1/2)=−ln 2. “Linear in features” includes the bias here, so z=w₀+w₁x₁+… .

The idea in plain words

The model assumes log odds equals the weighted score. Score zero means odds one, hence probability one half. Positive scores favor class one; negative scores favor class zero. The curve gets flatter near zero and one. More confidence does not require changing the predicted class.

Now read the math

Let z=w·x. The course assumption is about log odds, not probability itself:

ln⁡p1−p=z\ln\frac{p}{1-p}=zln1−pp​=z

Undo ln; then solve for p:

p1−p=ez\frac{p}{1-p}=e^z1−pp​=ez
p=ez(1−p)=ez−ezpp=e^z(1-p)=e^z-e^zpp=ez(1−p)=ez−ezp
p+ezp=ezp+e^zp=e^zp+ezp=ez
p(1+ez)=ezp(1+e^z)=e^zp(1+ez)=ez
p=ez1+ez=11+e−z=σ(z)p=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=\sigma(z)p=1+ezez​=1+e−z1​=σ(z)

The symbol σ is “sigmoid.” Class zero gets the complement:

1−σ(z)=e−z1+e−z=11+ez=σ(−z)1-\sigma(z)=\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\frac1{1+e^z}=\sigma(-z)1−σ(z)=1+e−ze−z​=1+ez1​=σ(−z)

Increase feature j by one, holding all others fixed. Its log odds rises by wⱼ:

Onew=ez+wj=ezewj=OoldewjO_{new}=e^{z+w_j}=e^z e^{w_j}=O_{old}e^{w_j}Onew​=ez+wj​=ezewj​=Oold​ewj​

Thus e^(wⱼ) multiplies odds, not probability. At all non-bias inputs zero, w₀ is the log odds and e^(w₀) is the odds. Day 11 p.15 informally calls w₀ an odds ratio; the defining log-odds equation gives this precise reading.

A tiny example, every step

Teaching example: z=ln 3 gives odds e^(ln 3)=3 and p=3/(1+3)=3/4. Add a weight ln 2 from a one-unit feature increase: odds become 3×2=6 and probability becomes 6/7, not 2×3/4. For a second teaching score z=−ln 3, the odds are 1/3 and p=(1/3)/(4/3)=1/4. Its class-0 probability is 3/4.

A probability threshold is a separate decision rule. HW6 uses p≥0.5, so z≥0 predicts class 1. Day 12 p.20 prints a strict >0 rule; exactly at zero we follow HW6’s ≥ convention.

Common confusions and the next connection

A probability estimate and a class prediction are different outputs. A weight’s units are per input unit: HW6 amount is hundreds of dollars. Next we score how good the probabilities are.

Cross-entropy: reward the true class

Source: Day 11 pp. 13–19; Day 12 pp. 18, 20–23; HW6 pp. 1, 3–4. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

Two weather apps both predict rain. One assigns rain 51%, the other 99%. A correct/incorrect mark treats them equally, but their probabilities make different claims. Cross-entropy judges the probability assigned to what actually happened, and strongly penalizes confident mistakes.

You need this first

First identify the true label y. Write p for predicted class-1 probability, and q for predicted probability of the true class. If y=1, q=p; if y=0, q=1−p. Σ means “add one term for each sample.” A product Π multiplies them. A mean is a sum divided by the number of terms n. ln is increasing, so bigger q gives bigger ln q; a minus sign reverses that order.

The idea in plain words

A true-class probability near one contributes almost zero loss. A tiny true-class probability contributes a large positive loss. “Most correctly classified” in these lectures means highest true-class probability, even when several samples share the same correct/incorrect label.

Now read the math

The y and 1−y factors act as switches:

qi=piyi(1−pi)1−yiq_i=p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}qi​=piyi​​(1−pi​)1−yi​

For y=1 this is p×1=p; for y=0 it is 1×(1−p)=1−p. Maximizing the product of true-class probabilities is the course likelihood objective. Taking ln turns the product into a sum without changing which weights maximize it:

ln⁡∏iqi=∑iln⁡qi\ln\prod_iq_i=\sum_i\ln q_ilni∏​qi​=i∑​lnqi​

Negate to turn maximization into minimization:

J=−∑iln⁡qi=−∑i[yiln⁡pi+(1−yi)ln⁡(1−pi)]J=-\sum_i\ln q_i=-\sum_i\big[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\big]J=−i∑​lnqi​=−i∑​[yi​lnpi​+(1−yi​)ln(1−pi​)]
L=JnL=\frac{J}{n}L=nJ​

J is a sum; L is a mean. HW6 P1 requests the mean, P3 and P4 request sums. Nats use ln, bits use log₂:

log⁡2q=ln⁡qln⁡2,Jbits=Jnatsln⁡2\log_2 q=\frac{\ln q}{\ln2},\qquad J_{bits}=\frac{J_{nats}}{\ln2}log2​q=ln2lnq​,Jbits​=ln2Jnats​​

Constant scaling preserves minimizing weights, but scales gradients. The same numeric learning rate then gives a different step. We use natural-log sums for training, exactly as HW6 P3’s provided gradient.

A tiny example, every step

Teaching example with two samples: y=[1,0], predicted p=[1/2,1/4]. True-class probabilities are q=[1/2,3/4]. In bits, losses are −log₂(1/2)=1 and −log₂(3/4)≈0.415037. Sum J≈1.415037 bits; mean L≈0.707519 bits. In nats, the sum is ln 2+ln(4/3)=ln(8/3)≈0.980829. The first sample is less correct, because 1/2<3/4.

Common confusions and the next connection

Do not rank samples by p₁ until you know their labels. Do not silently average a requested sum or mix bits with a natural-log gradient. Next we change weights to lower loss.

Slopes, chain rules and vector gradients

Source: Day 10 pp. 4–19, 29–31; Day 11 pp. 18–19. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

To improve a recipe, you need to know whether a little more of an ingredient helps or hurts. A derivative measures that local change. A gradient does the same for all weights, so we can improve a model without guessing every possible recipe.

You need this first

A function maps inputs to an output. A derivative f′(a) measures the limiting change in output per unit input near a. A tangent is the line matching the local slope. A partial derivative changes one coordinate while freezing the rest. ∇ (“nabla”) collects those partials in a column. A norm measures vector length: ||[a,b]||₂=√(a²+b²).

The idea in plain words

Positive slope means moving right increases output locally; negative slope means moving right decreases it. The gradient points toward fastest local increase, so descent moves against it. Zero derivative means stationary: it could be a minimum, maximum, or flat non-extremum. The optimization slides use minimum-oriented shorthand; zero slope alone is not a general minimum test.

Now read the math

Day 10 p.6 rules: a constant differentiates to zero; (xⁿ)′=nxⁿ⁻¹; (eˣ)′=eˣ; (ln x)′=1/x. Differentiate sums term by term. Products use (fg)′=f′g+fg′. Compositions multiply outer derivative at the inner value by inner derivative.

ddx(3x−1)2=2(3x−1)⋅3=6(3x−1)\frac d{dx}(3x-1)^2=2(3x-1)\cdot3=6(3x-1)dxd​(3x−1)2=2(3x−1)⋅3=6(3x−1)

Write sigmoid as power −1, then apply the chain rule twice:

σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1
σ′(z)=−(1+e−z)−2(−e−z)=e−z(1+e−z)2\sigma'(z)=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}(-e^{-z})=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}σ′(z)=−(1+e−z)−2(−e−z)=(1+e−z)2e−z​
σ′(z)=11+e−ze−z1+e−z=σ(z)(1−σ(z))\sigma'(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))σ′(z)=1+e−z1​1+e−ze−z​=σ(z)(1−σ(z))

For a vector example, freeze the other coordinate each time:

f(w)=w02+3w12f(w)=w_0^2+3w_1^2f(w)=w02​+3w12​
∂f∂w0=2w0+0,∂f∂w1=0+6w1\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_0}=2w_0+0,\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1}=0+6w_1∂w0​∂f​=2w0​+0,∂w1​∂f​=0+6w1​
∇f(w)=[2w06w1]\nabla f(w)=\begin{bmatrix}2w_0\\6w_1\end{bmatrix}∇f(w)=[2w0​6w1​​]

The vector chain rule is ∇g(aᵀw)=g′(aᵀw)a. To see why, expand aᵀw=Σⱼaⱼwⱼ. Its partial derivative in coordinate j is aⱼ. Multiply each by the same outer derivative and stack.

A tiny example, every step

Teaching example: f(w)=w₀²+3w₁² at w=[1,−1]. Partial slopes are 2 and −6; gradient [2,−6] has length √(4+36)=√40. With step α=0.1, new vector=[1,−1]−0.1[2,−6]=[0.8,−0.4]. Old loss=1+3=4. New loss=0.8²+3(−0.4)²=0.64+0.48=1.12.

Train logistic regression, one careful step

Source: Day 10 pp. 23–31; Day 11 pp. 18–19; Day 12 pp. 5–8; day12_ica cells 0–18; HW6 p. 3. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A nursery has survival labels for seedlings. It wants true outcomes to get higher probability overall. Gradient descent gives one coordinated adjustment from every seedling’s feedback, rather than fixing one case and forgetting the others.

You need this first

Recall pᵢ=σ(xᵢ·w), yᵢ is 0 or 1, and J is a natural-log sum. The residual rᵢ=pᵢ−yᵢ is signed prediction error in probability. Xᵀ swaps rows and columns; multiplying it by residuals adds each feature’s error-weighted values. α (sometimes η in lecture demos) is a positive learning rate.

The idea in plain words

A class-1 sample has r<0 and asks to increase its score. A class-0 sample has r>0 and asks to decrease it. Each request is multiplied by the input vector, so input magnitude matters too. Samples can pull the same weights in opposing directions. A step can lower total loss while worsening individual cases.

Mean loss divides this gradient by n. Bits also divide by ln 2. HW6’s displayed gradient uses a sum in nats, as do all our training steps.

Several classes: one vs rest and one vs one

Source: Day 12 pp. 2–4; HW6 p. 4(iv). Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A coffee shop predicts drip, latte, or cold brew. A binary yes/no classifier cannot name all three in one output. We can assemble binary decisions, much as a tournament uses pairwise matches to pick a winner.

You need this first

K is the number of classes. A pair is unordered: A vs B is the same classifier as B vs A. A vote is one classifier’s predicted class. A score is its confidence value. Binary logistic probabilities from separate models need not form a single probability distribution.

The idea in plain words

One vs rest (OvR) asks one question per class: “this class, or anything else?” Choose the largest score. One vs one (OvO) trains one classifier for each pair, using samples from those two classes, then chooses the class with the most pairwise votes in the course’s scheme.

Now read the math

OvR needs K classifiers. For OvO, class 1 pairs with K−1 others, class 2 with K−2 new ones, and so on:

NOvO=(K−1)+(K−2)+⋯+1N_{OvO}=(K-1)+(K-2)+\cdots+1NOvO​=(K−1)+(K−2)+⋯+1

There are K(K−1) ordered pairs. Each unordered pair is counted twice:

NOvO=K(K−1)2N_{OvO}=\frac{K(K-1)}2NOvO​=2K(K−1)​

OvR may have an imbalanced “rest” group; OvO’s classifier count grows quadratically. Day 12 describes OvO as often performing better; that is a motivation, not a guarantee. HW6 does not specify tie resolution.

A tiny example, every step

Three drinks: OvR is drip/rest, latte/rest, cold-brew/rest → 3 models. OvO is drip/latte, drip/cold-brew, latte/cold-brew → 3 models. If drip wins the first two and latte the third, votes are [2,1,0]; choose drip. OvR probabilities 0.7,0.4,0.1 can sum to 1.2: choose the largest, but these are not a normalized class distribution.

Check yourself

Common confusions and the next connection

OvO uses each pair’s data. OvR uses the other classes as a combined negative group. Next we build one multiclass model rather than combining binary ones.

Softmax: one probability distribution over classes

Source: Day 12 pp. 10–20, 25–26; HW6 p. 4. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

For a drink order, we want one probability for each possible drink and a total of one. Softmax converts class scores into that distribution. Imagine splitting a fixed pie among competing choices: increasing one share must take share from others.

You need this first

Each class k gets weight vector wₖ and score zₖ=wₖ·x. Σⱼ adds over all classes j. exp(z) means eᶻ. arg max means “the index of the largest value,” not that value itself. The indicator 1(y=k) is one when its statement is true, zero otherwise.

The idea in plain words

Exponentiate scores to create positive strengths, then divide by their total. Larger scores get larger probabilities. Adding the same constant to all scores leaves the shares unchanged. Multiplying all scores by a constant generally changes confidence.

Optional follow-on: why small weights help

Optional follow-on · not established Quiz 6 scope

Source: Day 12 pp. 21–24; Day 13 pp. 2–5, 10–12, 14, 18–23. Page numbers are 1-based PDF pages. Source links

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A polynomial can trace all training points yet swing wildly between them. Perfectly separating logistic weights can become absurdly sure. Regularization charges for large weights, balancing fit with restraint. This is follow-on material, not established Quiz 6 scope.

You need this first

Absolute value is distance from zero: |−3|=3. A norm measures vector size. L1 adds absolute entries; L2 is Euclidean length; L∞ takes the largest absolute entry. The course assumes L2 when there is no subscript. Squared L2 adds squares without a square root. λ≥0 controls penalty strength.

The idea

Lasso uses L1 and can make weights exactly zero. Ridge uses squared L2 and shrinks weights; it helps stabilize correlated features. Elastic net combines both. Choose λ through validation, like choosing k or polynomial degree. Too much regularization can underfit.

The data term here is SSE (n×MSE). For lasso, replace the penalty by λΣ|wⱼ|; its nonzero-coordinate derivative is λsign(wⱼ). At zero |w| has no ordinary derivative: Day 13 chooses sign(0)=0 as a subgradient. Fixed-step subgradient descent can chatter around zero rather than land on sparse optima.

Standardize inputs using training-fold statistics. Day 13 p.21 says not to penalize the intercept in practical fits. The earlier simplified formulas penalize all coordinates as written. If excluding bias, use a zero bias coordinate in the penalty gradient. Do not silently interchange objectives.

Teaching vector w=[3,−4]: L1=3+4=7; L2=√(9+16)=5; L∞=4; squared L2=25. At λ=2, ridge penalty=50 and lasso penalty=14. With η=0.1, the ridge penalty alone changes w by −0.1×2×2w=−0.4w, leaving 0.6w=[1.8,−2.4]. Add the data gradient for a complete step.

Before you start: how familiar is this?

Why this exists

A flowchart can ask “is the car inexpensive?” and refine its answer with more questions. A tree learns thresholds. A forest combines varied trees to steady predictions. This is follow-on material, not established Quiz 6 scope.

You need this first

A class proportion is its count divided by the node sample count. Independent draws multiply probabilities. A weighted average multiplies each group result by its sample share. A leaf is a tree endpoint. Cross-validation predicts held-out samples using models that never trained on them.

The idea

Gini measures label mixing: draw a true label and guessed label independently from the node proportions; how often do they disagree? Useful splits lower weighted child impurity. A tree greedily tests thresholds and features. Classifier leaves predict the majority class; regression leaves predict a mean.

Bagging draws n training rows with replacement, so a row can repeat. Forests additionally restrict each split to a random subset of features, then vote (classification) or average (regression). A bootstrap bag is not a cross-validation fold. Choose depth using held-out performance, because deep trees can overfit.

Worked example

At the alternative split ≤22, left has 4 yielding and 1 non-yielding: G=1−(4/5)²−(1/5)²=8/25. Right is pure. Weighted G=(5/7)(8/25)=8/35≈0.228571. This is above 3/14, so ≤12 is better. A child can become more mixed if the weighted total improves.

Teaching forest votes [yield,yield,do not yield] predict yield. Course feature importance summarizes Gini reduction, not direction or causation. Check held-out performance first. Correlated features can share or obscure importance.
